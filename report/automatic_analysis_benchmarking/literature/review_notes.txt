LITERATURE REVIEW FOR THE SIX-ROUND CLIR ANALYSIS
Reviewed: 2026-11-15
Scope: primary papers on generated queries, multilingual retrieval, language
alignment, query selection, and evaluation. This is a focused review, not an
exhaustive novelty search. The suggestions below are hypotheses for analysis,
not findings from this experiment. Paper-specific evidence and our proposed
extensions are distinguished throughout. Bibliography: references.bib.

1. Gao et al. (ACL 2023): Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels
https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-long.99/
Evidence inspected: abstract; PDF Sections 3, 4.1, 4.4, and 5.3.
Known: HyDE generates hypothetical documents from a query and retrieves using
their embeddings. The paper studies averaging multiple generated-document
embeddings, optionally including the query embedding. Its non-English tests
use Mr.TyDi; they should not be described as equivalent to our multilingual
candidate-pool evaluation. Contriever/mContriever are central encoders, with
additional supervised-encoder experiments.
Implication: identify our exact single-passage, output-language-controlled
implementation. A result with our settings is not a verdict on every HyDE
variant. Generation-only-from-query is an established property, not novelty.
Potential extension: measure the marginal effect of hypothetical content over
a same-language translation, across heterogeneous contemporary encoders.

2. Wang et al. (EMNLP 2023): Query2doc: Query Expansion with Large Language Models
https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.585/
Evidence inspected: abstract; PDF introduction.
Known: query2doc generates pseudo-documents and combines them with the original
query, improving sparse and dense retrieval in its experiments. This differs
from replacing the query with a single hypothetical document. Its principal
in-domain tests include MS MARCO and TREC DL, with out-of-domain experiments.
Implication: do not conflate query2doc, query rewriting, translation, and the
implemented HyDE baseline. Benefits of generated information, including with
strong dense encoders, already have precedents.
Potential extension: report where replacement loses useful query constraints;
such an explanation requires inspecting original and generated texts.

3. Weller et al. (Findings EACL 2024): When do Generative Query and Document
Expansions Fail? A Comprehensive Study Across Methods, Retrievers, and Datasets
https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-eacl.134/
Evidence inspected: abstract.
Known: across eleven expansion techniques, twelve datasets, and twenty-four
retrievers, expansion benefits are negatively associated with baseline strength.
The authors discuss extra information versus added noise as an explanation.
Implication: neither "expansion can hurt" nor "weak retrievers benefit more"
is a new contribution by itself. A correlation across our eight encoders is
also not causal evidence that model size determines expansion utility.
Potential extension: test whether this pattern remains after controlling output
language, corpus, and availability, and whether translation has a different
strength-dependent response from hypothetical passage generation.

4. Roy et al. (EMNLP 2020): LAReQA: Language-Agnostic Answer Retrieval from a
Multilingual Pool
https://aclanthology.org/2020.emnlp-main.477/
Evidence inspected: abstract; paper discussion of language bias.
Known: mixed-language candidate pools require stronger alignment than retrieval
into a single-language pool. Same-language preference can outrank cross-language
relevance, and performance under weaker alignment tests does not necessarily
predict multilingual-pool performance.
Implication: distinguish monolingual, cross-lingual target-language, and pooled
multilingual settings. Same-language bias and the need for pooled evaluation
are established findings.
Potential extension: quantify how output language changes recovery of the
canonical source versus coverage of its accepted language versions. Interpret
differences in light of the explicit relevance unit and number of positives.

5. Yang, Jiang, and Baldwin (MRL 2024): Language Bias in Multilingual Information
Retrieval: The Nature of the Beast and Mitigation Methods
https://aclanthology.org/2024.mrl-1.23/
Evidence inspected: abstract.
Known: semantically equivalent queries in different languages can yield
different rankings over a common multilingual collection. The work compares
traditional and neural retrieval and proposes a training loss for mitigation.
Implication: ranking instability across translation languages is not itself
new. A macro mean can conceal language-conditioned disparities.
Potential extension: quantify both effectiveness and ranked-result overlap,
and distinguish a changed language-version ranking from a changed underlying
source-document ranking. Do not assume a translation is perfectly equivalent
without checking examples and entity/constraint preservation.

6. Park et al. (Findings ACL 2026): Enhancing Multilingual RAG Systems with
Debiased Language Preference-Guided Query Fusion
https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-acl.1353/
Evidence inspected: abstract.
Known: DeLP explicitly accounts for exposure, gold availability, and cultural
priors when evaluating language preference. In their setting, apparent English
advantage is substantially explained by evidence distribution. DELTA exploits
query-document language alignment in multilingual retrieval and generation.
Implication: claiming that English is not always the best pivot, or that
same-language alignment matters, is insufficient novelty. Corpus composition
and relevance availability must be accounted for before calling a pattern
intrinsic model bias.
Potential extension: our parallel legal sources and accepted chemistry pairs
may support a narrower controlled diagnostic, provided matched-language
availability is explicitly enforced and domain differences are retained.

7. Zhu, Ming, and Kan (ACL 2026): When Does Mixing Help? Analyzing Query
Embedding Interpolation in Multilingual Dense Retrieval
https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1455/
Evidence inspected: abstract.
Known: interpolation of parallel query-language embeddings on mMARCO produces
structured target-language-dependent effects. The main BGE-M3 analysis finds
English-related asymmetry; the paper also reports robustness across model
families and scales.
Implication: language mixtures, English asymmetry, and multilingual query
complementarity already have direct prior art. Our independently evaluated
translation languages are not an implemented fusion algorithm.
Potential extension: examine whether the best single language changes with
domain and encoder, and compare any retrospective language oracle with a
fixed policy and a genuinely held-out selection policy.

8. Macmillan-Scott, Goworek, and Ozyigit (arXiv 2025): Generative Query Expansion
with Multilingual LLMs for Cross-Lingual Information Retrieval
https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.19325
https://arxiv.org/html/2511.19325v1
Evidence inspected: experimental outline; results; limitations.
Known: three multilingual generators, multiple prompting strategies, translation
versus expansion ordering, and query-plus-expansion versus expansion alone are
studied on CLIRMatrix and mMARCO. Query format, language, and script matter.
Crucial scope: its stated limitations identify BM25 as the evaluated retriever
and the original query as the sole baseline; translation and expansion effects
are therefore not independently isolated.
Potential extension: directly address that limitation with our paired
HyDE-minus-translation comparison in the SAME output language across eight
dense encoders. Do not claim the first multilingual generative expansion study.

9. Valentini, Kozlowski, and Lariviere (MRL 2025): CLIRudit: Cross-Lingual
Information Retrieval of Scientific Documents
https://aclanthology.org/2025.mrl-main.16/
Evidence inspected: abstract.
Known: an English-French academic benchmark uses author-written keywords and
abstracts. Dense retrieval without translation is competitive in that setting;
document translation and sparse methods also offer useful alternatives.
Implication: academic CLIR, translation ablations, and effectiveness-efficiency
tradeoffs have prior art. Its human-authored keyword queries and its document
translation condition differ from our selected synthetic questions and fixed
corpus embeddings; raw scores should not be compared across the datasets.
Potential extension: compare specialist versus general-purpose encoders within
our own matched chemistry, legal, and institutional corpora, with explicit
limits on transferring conclusions to real academic search.

10. Shanian et al. (EMNLP Industry 2026): Better Together: Complementary Query
Rewriting Under a Strong RAG Baseline
https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-industry.143/
Evidence inspected: abstract.
Known: a fixed BGE/reranking/diversification pipeline is studied on three
datasets. Rewriting strategies can make complementary errors, fusion can also
hurt, and confidence-gated rewriting is evaluated as a cost-aware simulation.
Implication: oracle complementarity, query routing, and reducing generation
calls by selective rewriting are established ideas. Post-hoc unions are not
equivalent to a deployable top-k ranking or an implemented budget-aware router.
Potential extension: evaluate whether cheap pre-generation features can choose
between original, translation, and HyDE on held-out source documents. Count
all variants that must be generated or embedded to obtain a routing feature.

11. Zhang et al. (Findings EMNLP 2024): Exploring the Best Practices of Query
Expansion with Large Language Models
https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.103/
Evidence inspected: abstract and PDF introduction.
Known: MuGI integrates multiple generated references with the original query
and studies weighting, integration, additional samples, and feedback. It reports
benefits over a range of model sizes, including small retrievers.
Implication: a blanket conclusion that stronger models never benefit from
expansion would conflict with other experimental evidence. The expansion
formulation and integration procedure matter; our single-passage experiment
does not test MuGI's complete method.
Potential extension: position strong-model degradation as conditional on the
implemented replacement strategy, corpus, and language rather than a universal
law about LLM augmentation.

12. Enevoldsen et al. (2025): MMTEB: Massive Multilingual Text Embedding Benchmark
https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13595
Evidence inspected: abstract and arXiv version metadata.
Known: broad embedding evaluation spans hundreds of tasks and many languages,
including long-document and instruction-following settings. Model size alone
does not establish uniformly superior performance across the benchmark.
Implication: MTEB-style result serialization does not make these custom tasks
an official MTEB leaderboard submission. Three corpora and five query output
languages do not establish universal multilingual model rankings.
Potential extension: provide a reproducible complementary retrieval diagnostic
with explicit corpus composition, task definitions, model revisions, and
per-query outputs, suitable for later integration into broader benchmarks.

PRIORITY QUESTIONS FOR THE SIX ROUNDS (PROPOSED ANALYSIS, NOT RESULTS)

A. Separate translation from hypothetical content.
   Primary contrast: HyDE(language L) minus translated-query(language L), with
   the same question, encoder, candidate pool, qrels, k, and eligible cohort.
   Secondary contrasts: both variants versus the original query. A gain over
   the original alone cannot identify whether translation or extra content
   supplies the gain. Report identity translations separately when applicable.

B. Separate information recovery from language-version coverage.
   Derive the relation between Hit@k, source recall, and document-version
   recall from the qrels. If each query has exactly one canonical gold source,
   source Recall@k may equal source Hit@k by definition. That equality is not
   a discovered empirical law. Plot version coverage conditional on recovering
   the source, and stratify by the number of available relevant versions.
   Compare source-level and version-level ranking overlap as descriptive
   diagnostics. Do not label unjudged non-source documents semantically
   irrelevant without additional relevance annotation.

C. Diagnose language alignment with availability controls.
   For fixed target-language retrieval, compare query output matching the
   target language against nonmatching outputs on identical questions. For
   pooled retrieval, examine both all eligible data and the subset with the
   same accepted gold-language availability. Keep corpus-specific results:
   legal parallel documents and directly accepted chemistry translation pairs
   need not induce equivalent positive sets. A pooled English advantage can
   reflect composition; a within-question output-language intervention is
   stronger evidence than comparing different original-language questions.

D. Find useful heterogeneity, not just a large mean gain.
   Show paired win/tie/loss rates and the severity of losses. Test whether
   method rankings reverse by corpus, target language, model, baseline
   difficulty, or original-query language. Large relative gains near a weak
   baseline require absolute percentage-point changes alongside denominators.
   Model size, training objective, architecture, and language coverage are
   confounded among the eight models; observed associations are not causal
   scaling laws. Generated length is also not an exogenous intervention.

E. Turn oracle headroom into a realistic decision problem.
   An oracle selecting the best method/language using test qrels is an upper
   bound, not a retriever that can be deployed. A fixed language/model policy
   selected on the full test set is also optimistically biased. Prefer
   question/document-grouped cross-validation, or leave-one-corpus-out checks,
   with policy fitting and threshold choice entirely inside training folds.
   Report the oracle separately and do not call its score a new baseline.
   A router using generated-text properties has already incurred generation
   cost. Score scales are not automatically comparable across encoders.

F. Preserve cost and latency interpretation.
   Distinguish generation calls, cold encoder work, cached reuse, and shared
   corpus preparation. The same generated text reused by eight retrievers is
   one actual generation expense, but its original cost remains relevant to
   an independently deployed pipeline. Include failed attempts in paid-call
   accounting. Scheduler wait and retry wall-clock time are not intrinsic
   retrieval latency. Unknown GPU prices cannot become zero monetary cost.
   A Pareto frontier is conditional on the measured/configured cost boundary.

RESEARCH CLAIMS TO AVOID WITHOUT FURTHER EVIDENCE

- First demonstration that LLM expansion, translation, or English pivoting can
  fail; first observation of same-language bias; first query-routing oracle.
- Treating any new combination of existing components as a novel algorithm
  when the contribution actually consists of controlled empirical diagnosis.
- Calling the five output languages representative of low-resource languages;
  most are high-resource, and Chinese also differs in script.
- Claiming downstream answer quality, factuality, or hallucination reduction
  from retrieval-only metrics. This experiment evaluates retrieved documents.
- Treating the selected highest-score synthetic questions as a random sample
  of user information needs. The quality score, generator tie preference, and
  source-document construction should be disclosed as sampling choices.
- Calling six adaptive exploratory rounds six independent confirmations.
  Distinguish exploration from confirmation, retain negative findings, and
  disclose which comparisons were chosen after inspecting earlier figures.
- Claiming robust across-domain generalization from only three corpora, or
  independent observations from repeated views of the same 638 questions.

POTENTIALLY USEFUL CONTRIBUTION FRAMING, CONDITIONAL ON THE RESULTS

A controlled diagnostic of what generation adds beyond translation in dense
cross-lingual retrieval, showing how conclusions depend on the relevance unit,
available language versions, retriever family, and corpus. Pair this with
transparent cost accounting and practical, held-out selection rules if they
actually transfer. The value would be the controlled contrasts and actionable
failure boundaries, not an unqualified claim that one baseline is universally
best. Novelty should be rechecked against the concrete final claims after all
six rounds, including the 2026 papers above.

FOLLOW-UP AFTER ROUND 1: NON-INDEX QUERY LANGUAGE AND METRIC REVERSAL
Added 2026-11-15. This section supersedes a language-bridge interpretation of
the initial German-translation heatmap pattern.

Question and current observation
The first-round UN heatmap suggested that German translation can improve
nDCG@10 for several encoders even though the searched UN corpus and selected
original queries have no German versions. The subsequent paired analysis
reported by the analysis lead gives a critical qualification: German-versus-
English translation has lower Hit@10 for all eight encoders. For E5, the
reported nDCG@10 is .2142 versus .1761 while Hit@10 is .5830 versus .6721;
for Gemma, nDCG@10 is .3976 versus .3730 while Hit@10 is .7166 versus .7976.
These are descriptive values, not significance claims. There are four positive
language versions per UN query. The proposed claim is therefore about system
selection changing with the retrieval objective, not a discovered German
semantic-bridge mechanism. Cite the round tables as the numerical authority.

Bounded literature check and close precedents
The search covered dense-retrieval pivot/query-language terms, transitive CLIR,
language bias, multilingual equivalent-answer evaluation, and parallel-corpus
metrics. This is a bounded review, not evidence that no exact precedent exists.

1. Classical pivot CLIR: Kishida and Kando, CLEF working notes 2003.
https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1169/CLEF2003wn-adhoc-KishidaEt2003.pdf
The experiment translates German queries through English into Italian and
retrieves Italian documents, with feedback to refine translation. It does not
directly embed a third-language query against unchanged target-language
documents. Thus third-language bridging has longstanding precedent, but this
particular implementation is not a direct precedent for our observed pattern.
Prefer the literal phrase "query translation into a language absent from the
index" over an unqualified claim about discovering pivot retrieval.

2. LAReQA, Roy et al., EMNLP 2020, sections 3.2 and 5.
https://aclanthology.org/2020.emnlp-main.477/
The evaluation rewards multiple correct language-equivalent answers using
mAP; it also examines a pool with all but one correct target removed. This is
close prior for explicitly distinguishing multilingual-equivalent coverage
from single-target retrieval, although it does not establish our translation-
language/source-success reversal.

3. SHIFT, Jang et al., Findings EMNLP 2026, section 4.3.
https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-emnlp.109/
https://arxiv.org/html/2606.18801v1
SHIFT evaluates translated gold-context versions as positives and introduces
Target-Languages Recall@k: recall restricted to relevant documents whose
language differs from the query. This isolates cross-language coverage from
query-language dominance. The method shifts document embeddings at indexing;
our experiment instead changes query language. Its coverage metric does not
measure whether any one version of the canonical source was found. For our
translation comparison, a language-relative gold subset would change with
the treatment: German has no same-language positive, whereas English does.
Consequently, applying that metric naively to compare output languages would
introduce changing denominators. Retain fixed source and version qrels for the
main comparison; describe any target-only subset by a fixed language rule.

4. Query embedding interpolation, Zhu et al., ACL 2026, section 4.5.
https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1455/
The paper reports 11 settings where mixing improves Recall@10 but lowers
nDCG@10. Metric-dependent effects of language intervention are therefore
already reported. Their contrast concerns coverage versus ordering; ours
concerns any-source success versus multiple parallel gold versions. Do not
claim the first multilingual metric reversal or the first useful query mix.

5. Park and Lee, Findings ACL 2025: Investigating Language Preference of
Multilingual RAG Systems.
https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.295/
The paper separates retrieval language preferences from downstream answer
quality and reports that stronger language preference need not imply better
generation. It reinforces objective separation, but is not evidence for our
specific retrieval-only reversal. Our study cannot infer answer quality.

Useful algebra and interpretation (derived from our evaluation definitions)
Let N_q be the number of accepted source-language versions retrieved in the
same top-k document-version ranking, and m_q the available positive count.
With one canonical gold source, H_q = 1[N_q > 0] and R_q = N_q / m_q.
For a fixed m, mean(R) = mean(H) * mean(N | H=1) / m. More generally,
mean(R) = mean(H) * mean(N/m_q | H=1). The latter remains valid when positive
counts vary. This identity explains how lower source success and greater
conditional coverage can coexist; the identity is not a research discovery.
nDCG also equals zero on misses, so its mean is Hit times mean(nDCG | Hit=1).
Its conditional component combines relevant-version count and rank discounts.
Do not attribute an nDCG reversal to count alone without checking positions.

If source recall is computed by mapping the SAME top-k version list to source
IDs, it equals Hit here because each query has one accepted source. Ranking
all canonical sources after deduplicating both relevant and irrelevant versions
defines a different task and cutoff. Do not silently switch these definitions,
or infer a complete top-100 source ranking from only 100 saved version results.

Most useful next evidence
- Show paired German-minus-English deltas with source-cluster uncertainty for
  both objectives, and the full eight-model sign pattern without calling the
  models independent replications. State that German was selected after
  inspecting the language heatmaps.
- Decompose outcomes into both-hit, German-only-hit, English-only-hit, neither;
  report conditional version count and discounted gain within the both-hit
  subset. Comparing each method's own hit subset alone mixes different questions.
- Check first relevant rank, second/third/fourth relevant ranks, and version
  language distribution. This separates lost source coverage from greater
  multilingual replication on sources already found.
- Keep four-gold UN comparisons separate from sparse chemistry translation
  pairs; include positive-count strata if combining corpora.
- Preserve fixed settings, same questions, shared translated strings across
  encoders, and the same qrels. A non-index language advantage in nDCG is not
  intrinsic language neutrality, especially with one translation sample per
  language and no independent human check of semantic preservation.

INDEPENDENT CRITIQUE OF THE ROUND 1 REVIEW AND PROTOCOL

The review appropriately labels five-language averages as diagnostics, retains
original and translation controls, exposes missing language cells, and limits
claims to synthetic score-selected questions. The protocol already specifies
paired source-cluster analysis and oracle separation. The following points
should remain explicit in later synthesis:

A. "German is strong" needs an objective in the sentence. The new Hit reversal
   makes "German improves UN retrieval" too broad. A useful narrower finding
   is that language choice can redistribute success across questions versus
   language equivalents; which outcome matters depends on the application.

B. HyDE-minus-same-language-translation is an essential baseline contrast,
   but it is not a pure causal effect of hypothetical content. The audit shows
   that HyDE uses document encoding while original/translation use query
   encoding, with different adapters or prefixes for four encoders. That is
   the implemented pipeline comparison; isolate encoder role only in a later
   authorized ablation. German-versus-English translation uses the same role,
   so this particular role confound does not explain that language contrast.

C. Translation into the original language may be an identity/no-op control,
   not a newly generated paraphrase. Language-averaged changes then mix true
   translation with no-op cases in proportions set by the selected-query
   distribution. Keep these strata visible; do not silently drop them.
   Near-tie identity discrepancies should be shown as a sensitivity analysis,
   not treated as independent language effects or hidden by rounding.

D. Legal whole-document/section units and chemistry abstracts differ in length
   and chunk count. Max-chunk scoring introduces different numbers of chances
   for a high score. A corpus-level reversal is descriptive; it does not prove
   that legal reasoning benefits more from HyDE. Report the granularity and
   discuss chunk-count distributions before supplying a domain mechanism.

E. Top-score question selection and unequal original-language cohorts limit
   comparisons across original languages. Within-question output-language
   contrasts are stronger. Preferred-generator ties can also shift query
   style and should remain part of sampling provenance.

F. Known source association is a valid declared retrieval target but does not
   exhaust semantic relevance. It also does not guarantee that every accepted
   source version contains an identical answer-bearing span. Retain the
   requested 638-question cohort; report quality-flag and no-span sensitivity
   without using it to replace winners or imply the main experiment is invalid.

G. Eight fixed models, five output languages, three corpora, and repeated metrics
   create many exploratory choices. Paired confidence intervals quantify
   uncertainty for a contrast but do not undo post-selection. Preserve the
   contradictory encoders and both control contrasts in the final claim.

Suggested cautious claim, pending the complete robustness checks:
"In a parallel UN document pool, selecting a translated query language by
language-version nDCG can favor a different choice from selecting it by
canonical-source success. The difference reflects both the fraction of
questions whose source is recovered and coverage/rank of its language versions."
This is a diagnostic claim, not a new metric, universal language recommendation,
or causal account of embedding geometry.
