WHY THE CHEMISTRY EXPERIMENT SCORES HIGHER
Reviewed 2026-11-16. Original-query dense retrieval; no paid calls or new retrieval.

Main finding
------------
The large aggregate chemistry advantage combines a different version-coverage
objective, a smaller pool, distinctive technical question wording, and different
language/question cohorts. The evidence does not identify one universal domain
effect. English-to-English EUR-Lex is actually better than chemistry for seven
of the eight embedding models. UN samples additionally show substantial source
identity ambiguity among closely related resolutions and longer retrieval units.

What was inspected
------------------
Computed features for all 638 selected source questions and their same-language
relevant texts. Checked all eight dense retrievers under language and quality
controls. For English text diagnostics, used all 46 chemistry, 55 EUR-Lex and
68 UN questions and their native relevant units. English source-frequency counts
use all 5,829 / 85,346 / 14,689 English units respectively.

Manually reviewed 21 seeded question/source/retrieval cases: eight chemistry,
five EUR-Lex and eight UN. The selection rule samples up to four English-native
hits and four misses per corpus (seed 20261116, sorted query IDs first). EUR-Lex
has only one English-native miss. These cases deliberately overrepresent failures
and are not a prevalence estimate. Inspection includes question, saved answer,
relevant source text and top-three/first-positive saved winning passages. Complete
source texts and inspection files are retained under cases/, with case_index.csv.

1. Most of the visual mixed-recall gap shrinks when measuring source success
-------------------------------------------------------------------------
EmbeddingGemma, all selected questions, equal original-language setting weights:

                         Chemistry     EUR-Lex        UN
Mixed Recall@10             86.43%       57.02%      35.06%
Mixed Hit@10                94.26%       85.67%      78.95%
Relevant versions/query       2-3            4           4

Chemistry has 184 questions with two relevant versions and 13 with three.
Both legal corpora have four for every question. Recall rewards recovering each
version; Hit rewards finding at least one. The chemistry-minus-EUR-Lex gap is
29.41 recall points but 8.60 hit points; chemistry-minus-UN is 51.38 versus 15.31.
About 70% of the numerical gap disappears under this change of objective. This
is not a causal percentage attributable to denominator alone: version count,
retrieval outcomes and pool construction differ together.

Evidence: mixed_version_and_source_scores.csv; fig01_metric_definition.*.

2. The blanket claim that chemistry is easier does not survive language control
----------------------------------------------------------------------------
English original question -> English target, one relevant unit, EmbeddingGemma:

                  Questions       Hit@10 / Recall@10
Chemistry              46                   86.96%
EUR-Lex                55                   98.18%
UN                     68                   83.82%

EUR-Lex beats chemistry in this setting for seven of eight encoders, including
BGE-M3, Qwen, Nomic, Granite and E5. LaBSE is the exception. Corpus size and domain
therefore cannot by themselves predict the observed ordering.

For EmbeddingGemma, restricting both query and target languages to en/es/fr
reduces the native-language chemistry/EUR-Lex gap from 8.60 to 3.98 points
(93.54 vs 89.56). The cross-language gap reduces from 14.83 to 7.04 points
(91.79 vs 84.75). This does not make question cohorts identical; it standardizes
the language settings. UN retains a larger deficit on these settings.

Changing setting-macro to query-setting-weighted recall changes the original
chemistry scores by less than one point. The n=1 chemistry cross-language cell
is a limitation, but equal-setting weighting alone is not the main explanation.

Evidence: english_native_all_models.csv; language_and_weighting_sensitivity.csv;
embeddinggemma_language_settings.csv; fig02_english_controls.*.

3. Many chemistry questions provide unusually selective retrieval clues
--------------------------------------------------------------------
One seeded example asks:
"Which patent publication describes a pyrethrin-loaded nanoemulsion prepared
by a low-energy oil-in-water method using a Tween 80 and Span 80 surfactant blend
at HLB 13?"

The accepted source MX-2025007921-A_en contains this precise recipe and ranks 1.
In the complete English chemistry pool, 'nanoemulsion' occurs in one unit,
'pyrethrin' in two and 'span' in five. The question supplies a very selective
combination of technical terms and numbers. Another rank-1 question names an
oligohydroxy compound; that word occurs in two English chemistry units.

With one comparable definition of rare (a nonnumeric content token present in
the relevant unit and in <=0.1% of the corpus's English units), the share of
questions with a rare matching term is chemistry 54.35%, EUR-Lex 40.00%, UN 0%.
The relative-frequency threshold avoids using the same absolute count against
very different pool sizes. It does not match document lengths or query topics,
and is not an estimate of the causal effect of terminology.

This collection called chemistry includes patent abstracts about materials,
mechanical components and devices as well as chemical compounds. The marble
decoration-plate example names PVC, heavy calcium carbonate and cooling warpage;
the accepted abstract ranks 2. These are specific invention descriptions.

There are also hard chemistry samples. A question asking which categories R1
and R2 may take finds the accepted formula patent only at rank 27. A question
about R12 in CH2OR12 ranks its source 66; another ring/formula specification
ranks 97. Similar Markush-variable lists recur across many patents, and the
questions can omit a more distinctive scaffold or identity. These counterexamples
fit a specificity/ambiguity explanation better than a universal domain advantage.

4. UN requires distinguishing many closely related institutional sources
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
The Haiti example asks what donors should do with pledges from the 2010 New York
Conference. Its designated source is resolution 2180 (2014), which ranks 30.
Rank 1 is resolution 1927 (2010): it explicitly mentions that conference and
urges donors to fulfil their pledges in a timely manner. Rank 3 is an earlier
Haiti resolution encouraging accelerated disbursement of pledges. The question
does not identify resolution 2180 or the year 2014. This is genuine competition
between closely related texts and a possible source-identification ambiguity;
the saved known-source qrels alone do not establish that each alternative is
semantically irrelevant or legally interchangeable.

These topics are widespread: 'haiti' occurs in 1,243 English UN units, 'pledges'
in 899, 'myanmar' in 1,103 and 'taliban' in 292. These are document-frequency
counts in the searched English corpus, not model explanations.

In the Taliban example, the question explicitly asks about a 2011 resolution,
but the accepted 1988 (2011) source ranks 11 and a 2014 resolution ranks 1.
The relevant source's winning chunk begins at character 21,725 in a 29,358-
character document; it contains Monitoring Team language and older resolution
references but omits the 2011 heading. Maximum-over-chunk scoring can select a
topically close passage without the distinguishing document header. This is
observed context in the saved passage, not proof that the omitted header caused
the ranking failure.

A Myanmar child-soldier question ranks its designated 2002 resolution 30, with
a later government letter's discussion/denial of recruitment ranked first.
Conversely, a question specifying the fifty-third session and Myanmar jails
finds its designated resolution at rank 1. The Ukraine question, which names
the resolution's territorial-integrity topic, also succeeds at rank 1.

An exhaustive English top10 passage scan finds a >=6-token saved-answer string
in an unjudged passage ABOVE the first accepted version for 9/68 UN questions,
4/55 EUR-Lex and 5/46 chemistry. Only 2 of UN's 11 English misses satisfy that
strict exact-string condition; the other seven are already top10 successes.
Do not misstate the count as nine failures explained or silently relabel qrels.

Examples: cases/un/q_c738132560256e7e0a97cb6518aa3f7b8cda1532570c4755cf449c88c54e68a5.txt

          cases/un/q_325409a0ac23ef16bbb1b1c4b9ce33d55257d9c0db74a4a7163aff4a17ce14db.txt

Counts: answer_bearing_unjudged_chunks.csv; answer_ambiguity_summary.csv.

5. EUR-Lex can have source ambiguity too, but its English queries are often easy
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
A question about identifying a conservation variety's region of origin across
Member States finds directive 2009/145 Article 8 at rank 1; the designated source
2008/62 Article 8 is rank 2. Both contain the saved answer that the Member States
must identify it by common accord. The question does not identify the directive.

However, four of the five sampled English EUR-Lex questions succeed, reflecting
its measured 54/55 English successes. Good-clinical-practice document retention,
equity market-price disclosure and seafarer dispensations map directly to compact
articles. The one English miss asks about recourse after refusal to open a Union
Registry account: account-opening articles occupy ranks 1-3, while the accepted
refusal/appeal article ranks 40. This illustrates related-topic confusion, not
evidence that all legal questions require harder reasoning.

6. Pool size makes a measurable difference under a defined counterfactual
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Using the saved original English scores/rankings, uniformly remove ordinary
corpus units until each pool has 5,829 units (chemistry's size). Keep EVERY
evaluated English source unit, not merely the positive for one query. All
questions in each corpus therefore share a pool containing their relevant units.
The exact expected top10 success under this sampling follows a hypergeometric
distribution. No embeddings or retrieval are rerun. Absent positives in saved
top100 are conservatively bounded rather than assigned a fabricated exact rank.

                 Original pool   Measured Hit@10   Expected after thinning
Chemistry               5,829            86.96%                    86.96%
EUR-Lex                85,346            98.18%                 ~100.00%
UN                     14,689            83.82%                    92.10%

For these inputs the censoring bounds agree at two-decimal precision. This
demonstrates the effect of deleting competitors while keeping queries, labels
and model scores fixed. It is an expected score under uniform corpus thinning,
not a new benchmark run, a hard-negative-matched experiment or a decomposition
of the original three-corpus score gap. These English cohorts have different
questions and keeping all evaluation units is an explicit sampling condition.

Evidence: pool_size_counterfactual.csv; pool_size_counterfactual_method.json.
The probability code passes exhaustive small-pool enumeration tests.

7. Factors checked that do not explain the gap on their own
---------------------------------------------------------
- "Chemistry simply copies the text more": not supported by the English
  diagnostic. Median question-content-word coverage is 75.34% chemistry,
  68.75% EUR-Lex and 90.48% UN. UN also has the highest question-trigram coverage.
  Term distinctiveness is a different property from raw overlap.
- "Chemistry has shorter documents than both": false. Median selected English
  relevant-unit length is 355.5 word tokens for chemistry, 153 for EUR-Lex and
  1,752.5 for UN; median chunk counts are 2, 1 and 6 respectively. Length/context
  fragmentation is plausible for UN, not an explanation of chemistry vs EUR-Lex.
  Document retrieval uses maximum chunk similarity; these are not whole-document
  single-vector embeddings. Do not infer that long UN documents were truncated
  wholesale or that length alone caused failure.
- "UN's questions are lower quality": simple audit flags do not support this.
  Clear-status fractions are 92.39% chemistry, 84.02% EUR-Lex and 95.55% UN.
  On clear-only questions, mixed Hit@10 is still 93.55%, 83.54%, 78.70%.
  Upstream grades/statuses are not independent calibrated difficulty measurements.
- "Chemistry benefited from selecting among more candidates": incorrect.
  Median candidate pools were 10 chemistry, 22 EUR-Lex and 20 UN. Persona and
  source-selection designs differ; this remains a synthetic selected-query study.
- "Chemistry has no duplicate distractors": false. Seventeen of its 46 selected
  English relevant units have another normalized exact-text English copy, and
  the highest-ranked unjudged result has identical normalized full text for
  13/46 questions. Similar patents/publication stages can compete too; do not
  merge or promote them without checking the source-association relevance rule.

Interpretation and limits
-------------------------
The defensible conclusion is that this patent-abstract benchmark offers a smaller
search space and many highly selective technical clues, while its mixed recall
requires fewer accepted language versions. UN frequently requires finding one
designated institutional source among related/repeated resolutions, with longer
units and less selective question vocabulary. EUR-Lex's lower aggregate does not
mean its questions are universally harder: English retrieval reverses the order,
and language availability/cohort composition affect the aggregate substantially.

Only the metric definitions and within-score corpus-thinning calculation have
direct mathematical attribution here. Content-based mechanisms are supported
by observed text cases and descriptive features, not an identified causal model.
There are no new significance claims. The original questions, qrels, rankings
and experiment settings remain unchanged. To assign residual effects causally,
use a prespecified matched-pool evaluation and independently adjudicate additional
answer-bearing sources; keep original results as controls and any new evaluation
or retrieval variants explicitly selectable.
